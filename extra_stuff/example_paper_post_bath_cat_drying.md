## Abstract

Bathing is occasionally necessary for domestic cats, yet little is known about how long a
cat's coat takes to dry afterwards or how cats behave while wet. We observed 48 privately
owned cats across three coat-length groups (short, medium and long) following a standardised
bath at a water temperature of 37 °C. Mean time to full coat dryness was 41 minutes for
short-haired cats, 68 minutes for medium-haired cats and 112 minutes for long-haired cats
when left to air dry at a room temperature of 22 °C. Towel drying reduced drying time by 31%
on average, and low-heat blow drying reduced it by 58%, though 11 of 16 cats in the blow
drying condition showed avoidance behaviour. Wet cats spent significantly more time grooming
(mean 46% of the observation period versus 12% at baseline) and sought warm surfaces more
often. Skin surface temperature fell by a mean of 1.9 °C within ten minutes of bathing and
recovered to baseline only after the coat was dry. We conclude that towel drying in a warm,
draught-free room offers the best balance between drying speed and cat comfort.

**Keywords:** feline, bathing, coat drying, thermoregulation, grooming behaviour, welfare

## 1. Introduction

Domestic cats groom themselves extensively and rarely need to be bathed. Bathing is
nevertheless recommended in some situations, such as after exposure to toxic substances,
for certain dermatological treatments, or for elderly and obese cats that can no longer
groom effectively. Owners commonly report that cats find bathing stressful, and images of
visibly drenched cats circulate widely online, but the period after bathing has received
little systematic study.

A wet coat loses much of its insulating value because water displaces the air trapped
between hairs. Cats are therefore expected to lose heat faster while wet and to compensate
behaviourally, for example by grooming to remove water or by moving towards warm areas.
How long this vulnerable period lasts, and how it depends on coat length and drying method,
has practical consequences for owners and veterinary staff.

This study had three aims:

1. To measure the time taken for a cat's coat to dry after a standardised bath.
2. To compare air drying, towel drying and low-heat blow drying.
3. To describe changes in skin temperature and behaviour while the coat was wet.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Animals

Forty-eight adult cats (26 female, 22 male; age 2 to 11 years, median 5) were recruited
through owner volunteers. All cats were neutered, healthy on veterinary examination, and
had been bathed at least once before. Cats were assigned to coat-length groups by an
examiner: short-haired (n = 18), medium-haired (n = 16) and long-haired (n = 14).

### 2.2 Bathing protocol

Each cat was bathed once in its own home by a trained handler with the owner present. Water
was held at 37 °C and a mild unscented cat shampoo was applied, followed by two rinses. Total
time in the water was limited to five minutes. The room was held at 22 °C (±1 °C) with
relative humidity between 40% and 55%.

### 2.3 Drying conditions

Cats within each coat group were randomly assigned to one of three drying conditions:

- **Air drying:** the cat was released immediately and allowed to dry naturally.
- **Towel drying:** the cat was gently blotted with two absorbent towels for up to three
  minutes, then released.
- **Blow drying:** a pet dryer on its lowest heat and fan setting was held at least 30 cm
  from the coat for up to five minutes, or until the cat showed avoidance, then released.

### 2.4 Measurements

Coat dryness was assessed every five minutes by touch at five standard body sites (neck,
back, flank, belly and tail base). A cat was recorded as fully dry when all five sites were
dry on two consecutive checks. Skin surface temperature at the back was measured with an
infrared thermometer at baseline and at 10, 30 and 60 minutes after bathing. Behaviour was
video recorded for 60 minutes after bathing and on a separate baseline day, then coded for
grooming, resting, shaking, hiding and contact with warm surfaces.

### 2.5 Statistical analysis

Drying times were compared with a two-way analysis of variance (coat length × drying method).
Behavioural proportions were compared between the wet period and baseline with paired
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Drying time

All 48 cats completed the protocol. Drying time depended strongly on coat length
(F(2,39) = 88.4, p < 0.001) and on drying method (F(2,39) = 61.2, p < 0.001), with no
significant interaction.

| Short       |   41 (6)   |    28 (5)    |   17 (4)    |
| Medium      |   68 (9)   |    47 (7)    |   29 (6)    |
| Long        |  112 (14)  |    78 (11)   |   48 (9)    |

Averaged across coat lengths, towel drying shortened drying time by 31% and blow drying by
58% compared with air drying.

### 3.2 Skin temperature

Mean skin surface temperature at baseline was 34.1 °C. Ten minutes after bathing it had
fallen by a mean of 1.9 °C (range 0.8 to 3.1 °C). The fall was largest in short-haired cats
that were air dried. Skin temperature returned to within 0.3 °C of baseline at roughly the
same time the coat became fully dry in every group.

### 3.3 Behaviour

While wet, cats spent much more of the observation period grooming (mean 46% versus 12% at
baseline, p < 0.001). Shaking occurred a median of 7 times in the first ten minutes and
rarely afterwards. Contact with warm surfaces, such as radiators, sunlit floor patches and
owners' laps, rose from 9% of the period at baseline to 27% while wet (p < 0.001). Hiding
was most common in the blow drying condition: 11 of 16 cats moved away from or struggled
against the dryer, and dryer sessions were stopped early for 6 of them.

## 4. Discussion

Coat length was the strongest predictor of drying time: long-haired cats took nearly three
times as long as short-haired cats to dry naturally. Because skin temperature recovered
only once the coat was dry, the length of the wet period is effectively the length of time
a cat is exposed to extra heat loss. For long-haired cats left to air dry, that period
approached two hours.

Blow drying was the fastest method but was poorly tolerated, which limits its usefulness
for most cats outside a professional grooming setting. Towel drying gave a meaningful
reduction in drying time and caused no avoidance behaviour in any cat. The sharp rise in
grooming and warmth-seeking while wet is consistent with cats actively compensating for
heat loss, and suggests that owners should give recently bathed cats access to a warm,
draught-free space.

### 4.1 Limitations

The sample was modest and drawn from cats already accustomed to bathing, so results may
underestimate stress in cats that have never been bathed. Dryness was judged by touch,
which is less precise than measuring coat moisture directly. All baths were conducted at a
single room temperature; drying in colder homes is likely to take longer.

## 5. Conclusion

After a bath, a cat's coat can take anywhere from about 40 minutes to nearly two hours to
dry, depending mainly on coat length. During this time cats lose skin heat and respond by
grooming more and seeking warmth. Towel drying followed by rest in a warm room is a
practical, well-tolerated way to shorten the wet period.
